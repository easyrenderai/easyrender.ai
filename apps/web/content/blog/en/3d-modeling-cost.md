A product 3D model costs the time and money it takes to deliver a file you can use. Add photo preparation, modelling or generation, review, revisions and export. Divide the total by the models that pass your checks to estimate the cost per approved product in a catalogue rollout.

![Game controller, half with a rendered surface and half in wireframe](/blog/illustrations/3d-modeling-cost/hero.webp "Editorial illustration made in Blender.")

## What are you paying for?

| Route                  | What to include in the quote                                        | Where the cost can rise                                        |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Generate from a photo  | Paid builds, internal review and any additional photo or correction | Several paid attempts on the same product                      |
| Commission a modeller  | Brief, agreed deliverables, revisions and final export              | Additional variants or editable source outside scope           |
| Reuse a supplier model | Permission to use it, cleanup and conversion for your store         | Dense geometry or material repair before the viewer accepts it |

Compare the fees against a specific deliverable. Choose the product and variants, the target viewer and the files you will keep. For example, Shopify’s guidance for [working with a 3D modeller](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/products/product-media/expert-3d-model) lists reference photos and measurements to supply at the start.

Give the supplier one acceptance check per important feature. Use a review list that says what should be visible from different sides, which materials must match and where the finished file must open. Confirm how many revisions cover defects against that brief.

## How do you compare the real cost per approved model?

Multiply your team’s hours for capture, review and corrections by its internal hourly cost, then add production spend. If you subscribe, decide what share of the subscription belongs to the pilot and apply the same allocation to every option. Then count only the files that pass the same acceptance checklist and open in the intended viewer.

| Hypothetical four-product pilot  | Budget units |
| -------------------------------- | ------------ |
| Photo preparation and production | 120          |
| Review and corrections           | 40           |
| Total spent                      | 160          |
| Approved models                  | 3            |
| Cost per approved model          | About 53.33  |

The calculation is 160 divided by three. Dividing by the four attempted models gives 40 and hides the rejected item. The example uses invented accounting units to illustrate the calculation.

When comparing apps, check whether credits cover textures, revisions and downloads on the plan you would use. For example, Meshy documents [task credits](https://help.meshy.ai/en/articles/10000507-how-many-credits-does-each-generation-task-cost) separately from [download access](https://help.meshy.ai/en/articles/10421033-why-can-t-i-download-my-model). Use [EasyRender pricing](/pricing) for the standard-build reference, then check the quote for your chosen quality before approving the build.

## What should count as approved?

Decide before the pilot. For a bedside table, the buyer should be able to recognize the two drawers, the gaps around their fronts and the wood finish. Open the exported GLB in the actual storefront viewer before approving the final deliverable.

This illustrates visual review, without measuring generation costs or time.")

The model shows both drawer fronts. The photo and the render use different lighting, so compare the timber finish again under similar conditions before accepting it. The [glTF specification](https://registry.khronos.org/glTF/specs/2.0/glTF-2.0.html) explains what is packaged in the delivery format.

| Check before approval                               | What to record                                         |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| The silhouette and main details match the reference | Views used for comparison and any defect to correct    |
| Materials look appropriate in the target viewer     | The texture problem and whether it requires a revision |
| The file opens on the product page                  | Format, file size and a mobile loading check           |
| The right variant goes live                         | Who approved the mapping and the specific version      |

For a simple product and a difficult one, note where the revision time goes before you extrapolate to the full catalogue. ## FAQ

### Should I budget for revisions?

Include revisions in both the quote and the time your team spends reviewing. Agree on how many rounds are included and what happens when a model misses a requirement in the original brief. Track requests for new finishes or variants separately so they are visible in the total.

### Can I use one pilot quote for my whole catalogue?

Start with a few items that represent the range of materials and shapes you sell. Record the pass rate and fixes for each group. A plain object and a reflective product with thin parts can require very different review time, even when both cost the same per attempt.

### What changes if I already have CAD files?

Check that you can use the files for your store, then decide which parts and materials the buyer needs to see. You may still need to simplify geometry and export to a format your viewer accepts. Open the resulting file and include that cleanup in the quote.

For product photography, start with one light and adjust it to the surface you need to show. Add a white card if a matte product loses detail in shadow, enlarge the source if a glossy product has a pinpoint reflection, and move the light across the direction of a texture to show its relief.

![Teapot shown as a solid object on one side and a wireframe on the other](/blog/illustrations/product-photography-lighting/hero.webp "Editorial illustration made in Blender.")

## Which setup should you start with?

| Surface                          | Start here                              | The first thing to check                                   |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Matte ceramic or paper           | One side light and a movable white card | Can you see the dark edge and still read the shape?        |
| Glossy plastic or glazed ceramic | A large, diffused source                | Does the reflection cover text or trace the curve cleanly? |
| Grooves, fabric or leather       | A side light angled across the detail   | Can you read the texture without losing the top or sides?  |

The pairs below are controlled Blender renders on the same ceramic test shape. We kept the camera and exposure fixed and changed one part of each lighting setup. They illustrate what to look for when you shoot your own product.

## Matte products: side light and a white card

Place the table next to a window with indirect light, or set a softbox off to one side. Take a baseline image, then bring a white card in from the shadow side. [Shopify’s product photography guide](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/products/product-media/product-photography) shows how to test the position of the reflector.

![Matte ceramic with a deep right shadow, followed by the same shape with a white bounce card](/blog/figures/product-photography-lighting/matte-setup.webp "Blender lighting simulation. Left: the side light alone. Right: the same light with a white reflector.")

Look for the edge that was difficult to see. In the render, the card opens up the right side while retaining some shading on the curve. If the product starts to look flat, move the card farther back and compare again.

## Glossy products: control what the surface reflects

A shiny bottle or glazed bowl can show the shape of your light source. Try a larger softbox or a diffusion panel. Move it until the highlight follows an edge or curve and leaves the label and important detail legible. [Adobe’s product photography guide](https://www.adobe.com/uk/creativecloud/photography/discover/product-photography.html) explains how modifiers diffuse the light.

![Glossy ceramic with a pinpoint highlight and the same material with a broader highlight](/blog/figures/product-photography-lighting/gloss-setup.webp "Blender lighting simulation. Left: a small source. Right: a larger source from the same position.")

In this pair, the larger source produced a broader reflection on the curved rim. The [Blender light reference](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/4.4/render/lights/light_object.html) explains how the area light represents a surface emitter. On a real shoot, review your reflection after moving either the light or the product, because both change its position in the frame.

## Textured products: test the direction of the detail

Choose a seam, grain pattern or row of grooves you want buyers to see. Aim across the detail, then raise or lower the light for a second shot with the camera still in place. Compare the texture on the front and any important surface facing away from the light.

![Ridged ceramic lit from above the side and from much lower beside the object](/blog/figures/product-photography-lighting/texture-setup.webp "Blender lighting simulation. Left: the light above and to one side. Right: a lower side light reduces detail on top.")

> **The lowest light can hide the very detail you need.** The higher angle makes the horizontal ridges distinct in this example. Choose the angle from the texture in front of you.

## Record the setup for the next SKU

1. Mark where the camera, product, main source and white card stand. Note where the light points, its height and its distance from the object.
2. Lock camera exposure and white balance for a comparison. Take a separate reference frame with a neutral card when color matters. Set the final exposure once you choose the setup.
3. Check highlights, shadows and the background at full size. Compare the final image to the product under neutral light, then keep one reference image with the setup notes.

See the [ecommerce photography guide](/learn/product-photography-for-ecommerce) for the rest of the product image set. If the same photo will be used to [make a 3D model](/learn/how-to-make-a-3d-model), check that the edges are easy to distinguish and that a hard reflection does not cover the design.

## FAQ

### How many lights do I need for product photos?

Start with one window or one light you can move. Use a white card to lift heavy shadows and a diffuser if a reflection is too small or bright. Add a second light when you can name a separate problem, such as a background that needs its own exposure.

### Why does the product color change between photos?

Mixed light sources or automatic white balance can shift color between shots. Turn off lights with a different color temperature, fix the white balance for your setup, and shoot a neutral reference card under the same light. Check the resulting image against the actual item before applying the settings to the batch.

### Should I light a textured product from below?

Try a light that crosses the direction of the texture, then compare two heights. Horizontal ridges can show better from above, while a lower side source can reveal a raised seam on another surface. Choose the frame with readable detail on the areas you need buyers to see.
